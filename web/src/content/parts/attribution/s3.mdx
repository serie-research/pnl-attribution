Factor attribution divides the P&L of positions already held. Signal attribution also needs to account for the policy that produced those positions.

A portfolio receives $K$ forecasts, the signals, but holds a single position.
The portfolio construction combines the forecasts with risk, transaction costs
and position limits. A signal that asks for a purchase may reinforce another
signal, offset it, or leave the portfolio unchanged. To attribute the
resulting P&L, we need to know how the signals become trades.

A policy that we can rerun lets us compare its outputs with different signals
as inputs. Known optimality equations let us split the joint solution directly.
@grinoldAttributionPerformanceHoldings1998 do this for an optimised portfolio,
with one portfolio per signal and one per binding constraint. A constraint
binds when its limit holds with equality at the optimum. Durin's variant in
section 3.3 spreads the constraints over the signals. Coalition methods need
many more reruns but no optimality equations. Because the methods use different
inputs, a signal's contribution means something different in each.

### 3.1 Stand-alone portfolios as proxies

A common method reruns the policy once per signal, with only that signal
switched on. It then fits the total P&L with a weighted mix of these
stand-alone P&Ls, by nonnegative least squares. Each signal's contribution is
its weighted stand-alone P&L, and what the mix cannot reproduce stays in a
residual.

Each run keeps the construction rules, including costs and constraints, and
gives the signal its own risk target: a chosen level of predicted portfolio
volatility. The stand-alone P&L therefore includes the trades the signal
generates: a fast signal's turnover, a slow signal's carried positions. The
method fits these P&Ls rather than the raw forecasts.

<Optional title="The proxy regression" subtitle="nonnegative least squares without intercept, residual">

Let $y_t$ be the total P&L in period $t$ and $\pi_{k,t}(\sigma_k^\star)$ the
proxy for signal $k$ at risk target $\sigma_k^\star$. We fit nonnegative least
squares (NNLS) without an intercept:

$$
\widehat\beta
=\arg\min_{\beta_k\geq0}
\sum_t\left(y_t-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k \pi_{k,t}(\sigma_k^\star)\right)^2.
$$

Writing $\pi_{k,t}$ for $\pi_{k,t}(\sigma_k^\star)$, signal $k$ is assigned
$\widehat\beta_k \pi_{k,t}$, and the residual is kept as a separate term:

$$
\varepsilon_t=y_t-\sum_k\widehat\beta_k \pi_{k,t}.
$$

</Optional>

The fit has no constant term, so it cannot absorb an average gain or loss, and
its nonnegative coefficients cannot reverse a proxy's sign. A losing proxy
receives a negative contribution if its coefficient is positive, and none if
the fit sets it to zero. Because the signals are correlated, trades offset and
constraints bind, the coefficients are not independent risk budgets in the
combined portfolio.

The one-step regression stops there. The iterated method rescales each
signal's risk target by its fitted coefficient, reruns the stand-alone
portfolios and fits again.

<Optional title="Iterating on the risk targets" subtitle="undamped update and fixed point">

In the simulation below, the optimiser adjusts risk aversion each period to
reach each run's risk target when the constraints permit it. The update is

$$
\sigma_k^\star\leftarrow\widehat\beta_k\sigma_k^\star.
$$

The update is undamped: each target is multiplied by the full fitted
coefficient. At a fixed point every surviving proxy has coefficient one, and a
proxy whose coefficient reaches zero drops out.

A scaled proxy can differ from the proxy of a new run at the new risk target, because the construction is nonlinear. The method therefore repeats the regression.

</Optional>

@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014 reports regressions on signal
portfolios as existing practice (pp. 2–3). He rejects them as a general method,
because binding constraints can leave a large residual. Section 3.3 describes
his alternative.

The residual collects the P&L of joint trades that no fixed nonnegative mix of
stand-alone P&Ls matches. Assigning it to the signals would make the
contributions add up without explaining more of the P&L.

A small residual means only that the proxies fit the total in this sample, not
that they are causal or that they predict what removing a signal would do.
When proxies are similar, their coefficients need not be unique.

### 3.2 Removing one signal from the full portfolio

Leave-one-out attribution asks what the portfolio loses when one signal is
removed. It runs the construction with all signals, then once with each signal
left out. @isichenkoQuantitativePortfolioManagement2021 [p. 134] scores a
forecast in this way, by how much the backtest deteriorates without it.

The leave-one-out contribution of a signal is the P&L of the full run minus the
P&L of the run without that signal, with both runs under the same risk
convention. A signal that is redundant with another can have a substantial
stand-alone P&L and a small leave-one-out contribution. A signal that helps the
combined forecast clear a trading threshold (section 3.3) can have the reverse
pattern.

Neither method guarantees that the contributions sum to the total, as Isichenko
notes for leave-one-out. When several signals jointly enable a trade, removing
any one of them removes the same gain, so the sum of removal effects counts
that gain more than once.

Both methods keep the market path, the cost model and the initial positions
fixed across runs. Only the signals and their risk targets change, and we state
which risk convention each run uses. The replay also assumes that removing a
signal leaves market prices unchanged. The remaining trades fill at observed
prices or through a fill model for passive orders, and the desk's own impact is
left out.

### 3.3 Using the optimiser's internal equations

@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014 uses the optimiser's own equations
to split its actual position into one position per signal, and the pieces add
up to that position. Each signal receives the P&L of its piece and a share of
the joint risk and cost. This works because, with the binding constraints
fixed, the optimal trade is affine in the forecasts. The method therefore needs
a known convex optimiser with a quadratic risk or cost term. It reads that
optimiser's positions, binding constraints and Lagrange multipliers.

<Optional title="What the split assumes: an affine map and frozen multipliers" subtitle="active set, intercepts, frozen multipliers">

With the set of binding constraints fixed, the optimality conditions are
linear equations in the trade and the multipliers. The forecasts and the
bounds sit on the right-hand side. The optimal trade is therefore affine in
the forecasts: a bound with a nonzero value, such as a position bound $M_c$,
contributes an intercept that does not scale with any forecast.

Durin's split has no separate intercept because the multipliers, evaluated at
the joint optimum, enter the shared matrices of the derivation below. With these
matrices held fixed, the recursion is linear in each
signal's forecast. Holding them fixed is a convention: the signal positions add
up to the joint position, but a signal's position is not the position the
optimiser would hold for that signal alone.

The inverses below require the matrix $Q$ and its constrained version
$\bar Q_t$, defined in the derivation, to be nonsingular on the free subspace:
the directions in which the binding constraints still let the trade move.

</Optional>

<Limits>

- **No optimality equations.** Heuristic position rules, black-box models and learned policies do not supply these equations. Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.4 describe rerun methods, and section 5.3 returns to such opaque policies.
- **Dynamic model.** @durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014 solves for the next trade only and covers the dynamic model of @garleanuDynamicTradingPredictable2013 through modified forecasts and matrices (pp. 4–5); constraining only the next trade while keeping the unconstrained value function approximates the full constrained dynamic program (p. 7).
- **Excluded constraints.** Combinatorial and non-convex constraints, such as round lots or a minimum trade size, are outside this treatment.
- **Linear and mixed-integer programs.** With no quadratic cost or risk term, a linear program's optimum sits at a vertex of the feasible set and is piecewise constant in the forecasts, so it has no linear split. Mixed-integer problems that carry state between periods, such as on/off decisions with start-up costs, are excluded too.

</Limits>

<Optional title="Derivation: one position per signal" subtitle="objective, recursion, P&L, risk and cost split, constraints, zero bounds">

Equation and page numbers in this block refer to
@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014.

Let $x_{t-1}$ be the previous position and $\Delta x_t$ the trade. Let $g_t^k$
be the expected return supplied by signal $k$ for the next period. The
combined forecast is $G_t=\sum_k g_t^k$. This sum assumes additive alpha
signals. For the features of a fitted model, removing a feature can mean
refitting, marginalising or imputing, and each gives a different attribution
[@covertExplainingRemovingUnified2021]. After the trade the position is
$x_t=x_{t-1}+\Delta x_t$. In the static model, the optimiser chooses the
trade that maximises the following objective. It subtracts a risk penalty and
a quadratic trading cost from the new position's expected return:

$$
x_t^\top G_t-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\,x_t^\top\Sigma x_t-\tfrac12\,\Delta x_t^\top\Lambda\Delta x_t .
$$

Here $\Sigma$ is the covariance of returns, $\gamma$ the risk aversion and
$\Lambda$ the matrix of quadratic trading costs. We replace $x_t$ by
$x_{t-1}+\Delta x_t$ and drop the terms that do not depend on the trade. What
remains is

$$
\begin{gathered}
\Delta x_t^\top G_t-\tfrac12\,\Delta x_t^\top Q\Delta x_t-\Delta x_t^\top Px_{t-1},\\
Q=\Lambda+\gamma\Sigma,\quad P=\gamma\Sigma .
\end{gathered}
$$

The matrix $Q$ combines the cost of trading and the risk the trade adds. The
matrix $P$ measures how the trade interacts with the risk of the previous
position. Durin writes the objective in this form (Eq. 5, p. 4), with the
static matrices of Eq. 7 (p. 5). The same form holds in the dynamic model of
Gârleanu and Pedersen, on which his paper builds, with different matrices $Q$
and $P$ and forecasts adjusted for each signal's decay rate.

Setting the derivative with respect to $\Delta x_t$ to zero gives the
optimality equation (Eq. 8, p. 6)

$$
Q\Delta x_t+Px_{t-1}=\sum_k g_t^k.
$$

The equation is linear in the forecasts and the previous position. If the
portfolio starts flat, as in the simulation of section 3.5, each signal starts
from $x_0^k=0$. A nonzero initial position belongs to no signal; one choice
carries it as one more component with zero forecast, which the recursion
unwinds. At each step we compute each signal's trade, then update its position
(Eqs. 11–12, p. 6):

$$
\Delta x_t^k=Q^{-1}(g_t^k-Px_{t-1}^k).
$$

The trade responds to this signal's forecast and the position it already
holds.

$$
x_t^k=x_{t-1}^k+\Delta x_t^k,
\qquad x_t=\sum_k x_t^k.
$$

The component positions sum to the actual position (Eq. 9, p. 6). The inverses
require nonsingular matrices (see the affine-map block); zero bounds come last.

Let $r_{t+1}$ be the vector of realised returns over the next holding period.
The gross P&L assigned to signal $k$ is

$$
\Pi_{t+1}^k=(x_t^k)^\top r_{t+1}.
$$

Multiplying every component by the same returns gives an exact gross P&L split,
as Durin notes after Eq. 12 (p. 6). For risk, write $V$ for portfolio variance
and $V_k$ for the share assigned to signal $k$ (Eq. 13, p. 6):

$$
V=x_t^\top\Sigma x_t
=\sum_k (x_t^k)^\top\Sigma x_t
=\sum_k V_k.
$$

Each share measures the component's covariance with the full portfolio,
including its interaction with the other signals. For quadratic cost $C$, the
analogous allocation is (Eq. 14, p. 7)

$$
\begin{aligned}
C&=\tfrac12\Delta x_t^\top\Lambda\Delta x_t=\sum_k C_k,\\
C_k&=\tfrac12(\Delta x_t^k)^\top\Lambda\Delta x_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

A component receives its share of the cost of the joint trade, including the
savings when component trades offset each other.

**Two ways to handle constraints.** @grinoldAttributionPerformanceHoldings1998
treat each binding constraint as one more source of P&L, next to the signals,
as Durin describes (pp. 2–3). In Durin's notation, the optimality equation
becomes (Eq. 17, p. 7)

$$
Q\Delta x_t+Px_{t-1}=G_t+\sum_c\theta_{t,c}.
$$

Here $c$ indexes constraints. The vector $\theta_{t,c}$ is the signed
contribution of a constraint's multiplier to the equation, including its
exposure direction. Running the same recursion on each source gives a
constraint portfolio beside the signal portfolios, hence a P&L per constraint.
It does not say which signals should bear that constraint's effect (pp. 7–8).

Durin instead folds each binding constraint into the risk and cost matrices
that every signal uses. A binding position constraint contributes extra
quadratic risk at the optimum; a binding trade constraint contributes extra
quadratic cost. Write $\bar Q_t$ and $\bar P_t$ for the resulting matrices.
Durin's signal-wise recursion (Eq. 24, p. 10, which reuses the recursion of
Eqs. 10–12, p. 6) is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x_t^k&=\bar Q_t^{-1}(g_t^k-\bar P_t x_{t-1}^k),\\
x_t^k&=x_{t-1}^k+\Delta x_t^k.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each signal's trades therefore include the effect of the binding constraints.
A binding trade limit slows trading in the
constrained direction. A position limit shrinks the aim portfolio (the target the trade moves towards)
in that direction, but can make the adjustment towards it faster. In Durin's value/momentum example, the no-short constraint affects even the signal that points away from the forbidden short position. The adjustment depends on each signal's position
from previous trades (Eq. 34, p. 13).

**From active bounds to effective matrices.** Let $a_c$ be the vector defining an exposure and $M_c$ its active bound. For a
sector exposure, $a_c$ has ones on the sector's stocks and zeros elsewhere.

At an active upper position bound, $a_c^\top x_t=M_c$. Durin uses the squared
constraint $(a_c^\top x_t)^2\leq M_c^2$ and a multiplier $\eta_c$ for it
(Eqs. 26–29, p. 11). A lower bound is treated in the same way when it is the
active one.

At the active bound, the squared constraint gives the same optimality equation.
Squaring a one-sided bound can change its feasible set, so the
nonnegative-penalty reading holds for bounds that reduce the trade or position,
in particular when zero is admissible (p. 14).

We remove the terms that are constant in the trade. For one position constraint,
the augmented objective is (Eq. 29, p. 11)

$$
\begin{aligned}
F={}&-\tfrac12\Delta x_t^\top Q\Delta x_t
      -\Delta x_t^\top Px_{t-1}\\
   &+\Delta x_t^\top G_t
      -\eta_c x_t^\top(a_c\otimes a_c)x_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last term penalises squared exposure. The matrix
$a_c\otimes a_c=a_ca_c^\top$ is an outer product. With $A_c=2a_c\otimes a_c$,
differentiation gives (Eqs. 29–31, pp. 11–12)

$$
\begin{gathered}
(Q+\eta_c A_c)\Delta x_t\\
=G_t-(P+\eta_c A_c)x_{t-1}.
\end{gathered}
$$

One position constraint adds $\eta_c A_c$ to both matrices. In the static
model, it adds $\eta_c A_c/\gamma$ to the covariance matrix. A squared trade
constraint, with multiplier $\mu_c$, adds
$-\mu_c\Delta x_t^\top(a_c\otimes a_c)\Delta x_t$ to the objective and changes
only $Q$ (p. 12).

For several constraints, let $\mathcal T$ index trade constraints and
$\mathcal P$ position constraints. Including the time dependence of the
multipliers, Eq. 31 (p. 12) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar Q_t&=Q+\sum_{c\in\mathcal T}\mu_{t,c}A_c\\
        &\quad+\sum_{c\in\mathcal P}\eta_{t,c}A_c,\\
\bar P_t&=P+\sum_{c\in\mathcal P}\eta_{t,c}A_c.
\end{aligned}
$$

Only position constraints change the term involving the previous position.
Inactive constraints have zero multipliers. For individual stock bounds, these
additions are diagonal, as in Durin's long-only example (Eq. 32, p. 12).

The solver supplies multipliers for the original linear bounds. Denote one
such scalar by $\nu_c$. Unlike the vector $\theta_{t,c}$ of Eq. 17, it
excludes the exposure vector. Let $s$ equal $+1$ for an
upper bound and $-1$ for a lower bound in the maximisation convention. The
attribution multiplier is (Eqs. 35–37, p. 14)

$$
\eta_c=\frac{s\,\nu_c}{2M_c}.
$$

This converts sensitivity to the original bound into sensitivity to its
square. The same conversion gives $\mu_c$ for a trade bound.

At a zero bound this division cannot be used directly. For one trade
constraint, define the adjusted forecast $\tilde g_t^k=g_t^k-Px_{t-1}^k$ and
let $a$ be its exposure vector. Applying Durin's rank-one inverse to each
signal gives (Eqs. 39–41, p. 15)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x_t^k
&=\left[Q^{-1}
 -\alpha\frac{Q^{-1}aa^\top Q^{-1}}{a^\top Q^{-1}a}\right]\tilde g_t^k,\\
\alpha&=\frac{2\mu a^\top Q^{-1}a}
                 {1+2\mu a^\top Q^{-1}a}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As the active bound tends to zero and $\mu$ tends to infinity, $\alpha$ tends
to one. This projects the trade onto $a^\top\Delta x_t^k=0$, along $Q^{-1}a$
(Eq. 42, pp. 15–16). For a position bound, we use its multiplier $\eta$ in
$\alpha$; the trade expression has the additional term (Eqs. 43–44, p. 16)

$$
-\alpha\frac{Q^{-1}aa^\top}{a^\top Q^{-1}a}\,x_{t-1}^k.
$$

It removes the previous position's exposure too, so the limiting component
position satisfies $a^\top x_t^k=0$. The infinite multiplier therefore has a
finite, well-defined effect on the attributed trades and positions.

**Spread costs and the no-trade band.** The portfolio trades only when the
combined forecast is large enough to cover the spread. For smaller forecasts it
stays inside a no-trade band, and no signal trades. Two signals can cancel
inside the band, or push the combined forecast out of it together. Each signal
pays the spread on its part of the joint trade and is credited when its part
goes against that trade. Durin reads this sharing of the spread as a voting
mechanism among the signals (p. 18).

<Optional title="Derivation: spread costs and their allocation" subtitle="effective cost, band condition, several assets, turnover and financing">

Equation and page numbers in this block also refer to Durin. He treats the
spread as an extra quadratic cost that grows as the trade gets smaller, and
gives every signal the same extra cost. We take a single asset in the static
model, with scalar return variance $\Sigma$ and scalar quadratic cost
coefficient $\Lambda$ (Durin's $\lambda$ in Eqs. 49–60). A positive spread
$\lambda_0$ is charged per unit traded, adding $-\lambda_0|\Delta x_t|$ to the
objective. For a nonzero joint trade, the effective quadratic-cost multiplier,
which Durin writes $\eta_t$, is (Eqs. 47–48, pp. 16–17)

$$
\eta_t^{\mathrm{spr}}=\frac{\lambda_0}{2|\Delta x_t|}.
$$

Smaller joint trades therefore face a larger effective quadratic cost. The
optimality equation becomes (Eq. 52, p. 17)

$$
(\gamma\Sigma+\Lambda+2\eta_t^{\mathrm{spr}})\Delta x_t
=G_t-\gamma\Sigma x_{t-1}.
$$

Spread costs enter the trade coefficient, leaving the previous-position
coefficient unchanged. Each signal uses the same $\eta_t^{\mathrm{spr}}$, with
$g_t^k$ and $x_{t-1}^k$ on the right. A nonzero trade exists only when
(Eqs. 50–54, p. 17)

$$
|G_t-\gamma\Sigma x_{t-1}|>\lambda_0.
$$

Inside this band, including its boundary, the combined forecast does not
justify paying the spread. The limiting effective cost is infinite, and every
signal's attributed trade is zero.

Stand-alone portfolios face their own thresholds and histories. A regression
coefficient therefore cannot, in general, line up their trading dates with
those of the joint portfolio.

For several assets, spread cost is assigned using the direction of each joint
trade. With $i$ indexing assets, its allocation to signal $k$, a positive cost
to subtract, is (Eq. 22, p. 9)

$$
C_{\mathrm{spread},t}^k
=\lambda_0\sum_i\Delta x_{t,i}^k\,
  \operatorname{sgn}(\Delta x_{t,i}).
$$

Here $\operatorname{sgn}$ is the sign function, with value zero at zero. A
component trading against the full trade can receive a negative cost
allocation because it reduces the net trade to execute. Subtracting these
spread allocations and the quadratic costs from each component's gross P&L
gives an exact net split. The effective costs shape the component trades. They
are not extra cash costs to subtract.

The same construction handles a turnover constraint, with its multiplier in
place of the spread coefficient. Financing costs and leverage constraints
penalise absolute positions instead of absolute trades. They become effective
risk terms and can force every component's position to zero (Durin, Sections
2.3 and 3.4; pp. 8–9 and 18, Eqs. 55–60).

### 3.4 Sharing value across coalitions

Coalition methods rerun the construction on every subset of signals, not only
single signals and single removals. The value of a coalition is the P&L of its
run. The Shapley value from cooperative game theory divides the gain of the
full set over the empty coalition among the signals.

The empty coalition, with no signal, needs a reference policy. The Shapley
contributions add up to the full P&L minus the P&L of that policy, and they
depend on it, as SHAP shares depend on their baseline in section 4.2. Below,
the reference policy does not trade, so the empty coalition earns nothing.

The Shapley value credits a signal with its marginal contribution, the extra
P&L when it joins a coalition. It averages that contribution over all orders in
which the $K$ signals can enter. A signal's stand-alone value is then its
marginal contribution when it enters first. Its leave-one-out contribution is
its marginal contribution when it enters last, and Shapley also uses every
coalition in between.

<Optional title="The Shapley formula and the empty coalition" subtitle="marginal contributions, weights, reference policy, risk convention">

Let $N$ be the set of the $K$ signals, so $K=|N|$. The value $v(S)$ of a
coalition $S\subseteq N$ is the P&L of the construction with only the signals
in $S$, under a given risk convention. The stand-alone value of signal $k$ is
$v(\{k\})$, and its leave-one-out contribution is

$$
L_k=v(N)-v(N\setminus\{k\}).
$$

The stand-alone proxy of section 3.1 equals $v(\{k\})$ only under the same risk
convention, so updating the proxies' risk targets defines a different game.

The value $v(\varnothing)$ is the P&L of the reference policy, zero here. With
a reference policy that trades, $v(S)$ is replaced by $v(S)-v(\varnothing)$.

The marginal contribution of signal $k$ to a coalition $S$ is

$$
\Delta_k v(S)=v(S\cup\{k\})-v(S).
$$

This is the extra P&L when signal $k$ joins the signals already in $S$. The
contribution of signal $k$ is

$$
\phi_k=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{k\}}
\omega(S)\,\Delta_k v(S),
\qquad
\omega(S)=\frac{|S|!\,(K-|S|-1)!}{K!}.
$$

The Shapley weight $\omega(S)$ is the probability that, in a uniformly random
order of the $K$ signals, exactly the signals of $S$ come before $k$: there are
$|S|!\,(K-|S|-1)!$ such orders out of $K!$. For each order, the successive
increments sum to $v(N)-v(\varnothing)$, so their average does too:
$\sum_k\phi_k=v(N)-v(\varnothing)$.

</Optional>

<PaperNote id="shapleyValueNpersonGames1953" look="RM-670 (1951), Section 5 (bargaining procedure), pp. 12–13">

@shapleyValueNpersonGames1953 defines the value by a random-order procedure:
each player receives its average contribution on joining the players already
present. Applied here, it divides the P&L of a coalition game with given
trading and risk conventions. Page numbers refer to the 1951
RAND memorandum RM-670, *Notes on the n-person game, II*. The 1953 book chapter
is paginated differently.

</PaperNote>

In an additive game the stand-alone values already sum to the total, and
Shapley returns them. Otherwise the gap between a signal's Shapley contribution
and its stand-alone value is the part of the interaction that Shapley gives to
that signal. The proxy regression, by contrast, fits only the stand-alone
series.

Exact Shapley needs a full backtest of the optimiser for every nonempty
coalition, and the number of coalitions doubles with each added signal. With
many signals, sampling random orders of entry can reduce the number of
backtests. Grouping signals into families, as in Owen's value, also cuts the
cost but gives an allocation different from Shapley's. Durin's
[internal allocation](#33-using-the-optimisers-internal-equations), by
contrast, needs no rerun at all.

On our synthetic histories with up to eight signals, no sampler reached the
target precision (defined below) with fewer coalition values than exact
enumeration. Owen's grouped value needed fewer values and stayed close to
Shapley, while the approximation that drops interactions above order two
performed poorly. Up to eight signals, exact enumeration was the cheapest way
to compute Shapley's allocation itself. Owen's value costs less but gives a
different allocation. With more signals, sampling becomes necessary, and its
error is worth comparing with the dispersion across histories.

<Optional title="Sampling estimators and their error" subtitle="KernelSHAP, history dispersion, sampling bounds">

@castroPolynomialCalculationShapley2009 average marginal contributions over
random signal orders. Among the samplers that
@mitchellSamplingPermutationsShapley2022 compare, one pairs each order with its
reverse to reduce sampling error. @owenValuesGamesPriori1977
averages only over the orders that keep each family together. KernelSHAP [@lundbergUnifiedApproachInterpreting2017] fits the contributions by
weighted regression on sampled coalition values.
@covertImprovingKernelSHAPPractical2021 improve this estimator by pairing each
sampled coalition with its complement.

We measure the distance between two share vectors as half the sum of absolute
differences between shares (the half-L1 distance), using the full portfolio's
P&L as the common denominator. History dispersion is the median pairwise
distance between exact share vectors from five histories. It describes the
dispersion across histories and is not a confidence interval.

Sampling error is measured over repeated draws on each fixed history.
@malekiBoundingEstimationError2013 bound the error of sampled Shapley estimates.
Those bounds cover sampling error only, not uncertainty about the trading
history.

</Optional>

<Optional title="The enumeration and sampling study in numbers" subtitle="coalition values needed, target precision, Owen, pairwise approximation" id="approximate-shapley-numbers">

With 4, 6 and 8 signals, exact enumeration needed 16, 64 and 256 coalition
values. Neither reverse-order pairs nor paired KernelSHAP attained the target
precision with fewer values than that. We measured precision against the
dispersion of exact shares across histories, which was 0.033–0.059 in half-L1
distance, and required the worst history's 95th-percentile sampling error to be
below a third of that dispersion.

For the same signal counts, Owen's grouped value needed 12, 28 and 60
coalition values. Its median distances to Shapley were 0.013–0.031 across the
tested sizes and risk conventions. These distances measure the effect of the
groups.

The approximation that removes interactions above order two is exact with fixed
$\gamma$, no position limit and no spread, because positions are then additive
(section 3.5). It performed poorly on these constrained examples.

The synthetic desk trades 50 assets on four signals, fast and slow versions of
two scores A and B, under a spread cost and a position limit. The scenarios
vary the spread, the number of signals, the innovation correlation (the
correlation between the signals' innovations) and the position limit. The
setup follows the figures.

All methods receive the same signals and returns. Durin gives an exact
internal allocation for this optimiser, so we use it as the benchmark for this
comparison. Agreement with Durin does not make a contribution the only correct
one.

We compare two Shapley games: every coalition gets the same risk target, or
every coalition uses the risk aversion $\gamma_t$ of the full portfolio at each
date. The figures label them “Shapley · risk target” and “Shapley ·
full-portfolio γ”.

The stand-alone proxy method stays close to Durin when the spread is small and
the signals are few. With a large spread, most of the variation of the P&L ends
up in its residual. With strongly correlated signals, the method usually keeps
only one signal. At the base configuration, its residual holds 42% of the P&L
variance and its half-L1 distance from Durin is 0.19, against 0.045 and 0.037
for the two Shapley games.

The spread is per unit traded, in multiples of $\mathrm{std}(G)$. Zero coefficients
are the average number of dropped or zero-P&L proxies, out of the number of
signals. Convergence requires every surviving proxy's coefficient to be within
$10^{-3}$ of one after at most 50 iterations. A run with no surviving proxy
does not count as converged.

Even at zero spread, the risk target rescales the portfolio each period:
removing a signal changes the risk aversion applied to the others, so the
stand-alone runs do not reproduce the joint run. Identical proxy columns can
also give non-unique NNLS solutions, so the fit may split identical signals
unequally.

The one-step regression's residual need not equal the total coalition
interaction of section 3.4, $v(N)-\sum_k v(\{k\})=\sum_k(\phi_k-v(\{k\}))$.
Regression absorbs part of the interaction into its coefficients, and the
nonnegativity constraint also affects the residual. The residual is what the
proxies leave unexplained, while the distance compares the signal allocations
themselves.

Costs vary little over time in this experiment: at the base configuration,
their standard deviation is 2.5% of that of gross P&L.

In a variant that fits gross P&L and then attaches each proxy's scaled costs,
the distance moves from 0.19 to 0.18 and the residual variance from 41.6% to 42.4%.
Separating costs therefore does not remove the construction interactions.

With fixed risk aversion, no position limit and no spread, positions are
additive. Gross P&L is then linear in the signal positions, and quadratic costs
add pairwise cross terms; Shapley and Durin coincide, including the allocation
of quadratic costs. This equality does not extend automatically to the
nonlinear portfolio: at the base configuration the gap is the 0.045 and 0.037
reported above. Shapley's contributions reconcile with the original P&L only if
the rerun of the full coalition reproduces the original portfolio.
