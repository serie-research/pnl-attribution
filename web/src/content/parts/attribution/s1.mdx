To assign amounts to indicators, we need a total and a reference. Against an
index, the total is the active return: the portfolio return minus the index
return over the same interval.

For a trading portfolio, one reference is a *paper portfolio*: the trades
intended at decision time, valued without execution costs. A market maker has
no such list of intended trades (section 2.5). We can also fix a valuation rule, such as marking every position
at the midprice, and explain the resulting P&L. We state the reference with
every result, because the contributions change with it.

An attribution writes the difference as

$$
\Pi-\Pi^{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_j \Pi_j+\varepsilon.
$$

Here $\Pi$ is the realised P&L, $\Pi^{\mathrm{ref}}$ the reference P&L, $\Pi_j$
the contribution of component $j$ and $\varepsilon$ the residual.

We report the residual as its own term, so that the reader sees how much of the
total the chosen model leaves unexplained. Because the residual is whatever the
components leave, the components and the residual always sum to the total, so
the equality alone says nothing about why the P&L arose.

### 1.1 What daily snapshots miss

@paleologoElementsQuantitativeInvesting2025 [p. 335] records positions at fixed
snapshots, such as each close, and splits realised P&L into *position P&L*,
what those positions would earn if held to the next snapshot, and *trading
P&L*, the rest. Intraday alpha, spread earned and execution costs all fall into
trading P&L, so explaining them needs the fills and the inventory path, or a
model of how the signals produced the trades.

<Optional title="Position and trading P&L in detail" subtitle="factor and specific P&L, a portfolio flat at every snapshot">

A factor model divides position P&L into *factor P&L* and *specific P&L*
(section 2.3). Trading P&L is realised P&L minus position P&L, so it holds all
gains and losses from trades between snapshots. If a portfolio is flat at every
snapshot of a daily attribution, its position P&L is zero and all realised P&L
is trading P&L, even though the portfolio carried inventory and factor risk
between snapshots.

</Optional>

### 1.2 Information and accounting interval

An attribution also depends on what was known when: exposures recorded before
returns test the bets declared in advance
[@grinoldActivePortfolioManagement2000, p. 479], whereas exposures fitted
afterwards, as in returns-based style analysis, test an explanation chosen with
hindsight. Currency P&L adds up across periods, while daily percentage
contributions compound and need a *linking* rule to sum to the period return.

<Optional title="Exposures, holdings and linking in detail" subtitle="ex ante and ex post, holdings-based and returns-based, compounding">

An exposure measures the portfolio's sensitivity to a factor. *Ex ante*
analysis uses exposures and a risk model recorded before returns occur. *Ex
post* analysis combines them with realised returns.

Holdings-based attribution uses observed positions. Returns-based attribution
estimates exposures by regressing portfolio returns on index returns, as in
Sharpe's style analysis (section 2.2), and these estimates may differ from the
positions actually held.

Currency P&L adds up across non-overlapping periods on a consistent accounting
basis, but percentage returns compound. The period return is
$\prod_t(1+R_t)-1$, not $\sum_t R_t$, so daily percentage contributions do not
sum to it. A linking rule rescales them. Working in currency avoids linking
but still needs reference prices and a rule to share interaction terms, such as
Brinson's in section 2.1.

</Optional>

### 1.3 Two kinds of indicator

Indicators also differ in the role they play in trading.

**Signals used to take positions.** A forecast enters the trading policy and
may change its trades. If we can rerun the policy, we can remove signals, run
them alone or combine subsets. These runs share one market path and one risk
convention, and what each signal earned depends on both. Section 3 shares the resulting P&L
between signals, and section 4 explains why that differs from sharing
predictive information between indicators.

**State indicators.** Spread, volatility and market order flow describe the
conditions in which gains and losses occur, and the policy need not use them as
inputs. Inventory and the desk's own orders are different: the desk's signals
produce them, so they belong with the policy, and their link with the P&L
partly reflects the signals.

We can first split the P&L in the fill records into spread and inventory P&L
(section 2.5), then relate each part to the state indicators (section 4).

The same variable can play both roles. A volatility forecast fed to the policy
is a signal, and removing it before rerunning the policy changes the trades.
Realised volatility used to compare fills in calm and volatile periods is a
state indicator; the comparison is descriptive.
