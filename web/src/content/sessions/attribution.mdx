## Introduction

This page reviews how finance and statistics attribute a trading P&L to signals
and indicators. It also shows where the methods disagree and which open
questions could become research projects.

No desk data is used here. Every number comes from a published paper, a
synthetic simulation or an illustrative example, and
[Code and data](#code-and-data) links the code or summary output behind it. The
page is written for people who work on or with an intraday trading desk
(traders, quants and risk teams), and it assumes basic linear algebra and
statistics.

A desk that tracks spread, volatility, order flow, inventory and its own
trading signals wants to know where its P&L comes from. A P&L decomposition
assigns parts of that P&L to signals, positions and costs, so the desk can
report what each one contributed. Whether to drop or resize a signal is a
separate question, and it needs other quantities (sections 3.2 and 5.2).

*Explaining* a P&L can mean two things. An explanation can point to an unusual
market condition, such as a wide spread or high volume when losses occurred. It
can also give each signal, position or cost its contribution to the P&L, as the
accounting and allocation methods of sections 1–3 do. Section 4 treats a
related question: what correlated indicators predict about the P&L.

A list of conditions tells the trader where to look without establishing a
cause. Three problems separate such a list from a P&L attribution.

**Amounts over an interval.** An attribution starts from an amount, positive
or negative, accumulated over an interval, and it sizes the part of that amount
that goes to each signal, position or cost.

**Scores and currency.** Several indicators can each raise a useful alarm, but
their scores are hit rates or classification rates, and rates do not add up to
a P&L in currency. Contributions that sum to the P&L need a specified total,
compatible units and a residual for the amount that remains unallocated.

**Overlap.** Volume, volatility and spread indicators often pick out the same
episode. If each indicator receives credit for the whole episode, the episode
is counted more than once. If only the strongest indicator counts, the other
indicators receive nothing, so a rule is needed to share the information.

factor methods come first, then signals and correlated indicators, and research
projects close the page.

## 1. What it means to attribute a P&L

<AttributionFigure title="Four choices define the attribution" desc="The bars show how the reference P&amp;L, components and residual sum to the realised P&amp;L. The bar heights are schematic. Select a choice to see which terms it fixes." alt="Realised P&amp;L minus reference P&amp;L equals the sum of the components plus a residual. The residual is reported as its own term. Four choices define the components: the reference, the accounting interval, the information and the kind of indicator. The reference can be a paper portfolio or positions valued at the midprice. The interval separates position P&amp;L from trading P&amp;L. The information choice is whether exposures were declared before the result or fitted after it. Signals drive trades, and state indicators describe market conditions. The sum alone does not show that the components explain the P&amp;L." />

To assign amounts to indicators, we need a total and a reference. Against an
index, the total is the active return: the portfolio return minus the index
return over the same interval.

For a trading portfolio, one reference is a *paper portfolio*: the trades
intended at decision time, valued without execution costs. A market maker has
no such list of intended trades (section 2.5). We can also fix a valuation rule, such as marking every position
at the midprice, and explain the resulting P&L. We state the reference with
every result, because the contributions change with it.

An attribution writes the difference as

$$
\Pi-\Pi^{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_j \Pi_j+\varepsilon.
$$

Here $\Pi$ is the realised P&L, $\Pi^{\mathrm{ref}}$ the reference P&L, $\Pi_j$
the contribution of component $j$ and $\varepsilon$ the residual.

We report the residual as its own term, so that the reader sees how much of the
total the chosen model leaves unexplained. Because the residual is whatever the
components leave, the components and the residual always sum to the total, so
the equality alone says nothing about why the P&L arose.

### 1.1 What daily snapshots miss

@paleologoElementsQuantitativeInvesting2025 [p. 335] records positions at fixed
snapshots, such as each close, and splits realised P&L into *position P&L*,
what those positions would earn if held to the next snapshot, and *trading
P&L*, the rest. Intraday alpha, spread earned and execution costs all fall into
trading P&L, so explaining them needs the fills and the inventory path, or a
model of how the signals produced the trades.

<Optional title="Position and trading P&L in detail" subtitle="factor and specific P&L, a portfolio flat at every snapshot">

A factor model divides position P&L into *factor P&L* and *specific P&L*
(section 2.3). Trading P&L is realised P&L minus position P&L, so it holds all
gains and losses from trades between snapshots. If a portfolio is flat at every
snapshot of a daily attribution, its position P&L is zero and all realised P&L
is trading P&L, even though the portfolio carried inventory and factor risk
between snapshots.

</Optional>

### 1.2 Information and accounting interval

An attribution also depends on what was known when: exposures recorded before
returns test the bets declared in advance
[@grinoldActivePortfolioManagement2000, p. 479], whereas exposures fitted
afterwards, as in returns-based style analysis, test an explanation chosen with
hindsight. Currency P&L adds up across periods, while daily percentage
contributions compound and need a *linking* rule to sum to the period return.

<Optional title="Exposures, holdings and linking in detail" subtitle="ex ante and ex post, holdings-based and returns-based, compounding">

An exposure measures the portfolio's sensitivity to a factor. *Ex ante*
analysis uses exposures and a risk model recorded before returns occur. *Ex
post* analysis combines them with realised returns.

Holdings-based attribution uses observed positions. Returns-based attribution
estimates exposures by regressing portfolio returns on index returns, as in
Sharpe's style analysis (section 2.2), and these estimates may differ from the
positions actually held.

Currency P&L adds up across non-overlapping periods on a consistent accounting
basis, but percentage returns compound. The period return is
$\prod_t(1+R_t)-1$, not $\sum_t R_t$, so daily percentage contributions do not
sum to it. A linking rule rescales them. Working in currency avoids linking
but still needs reference prices and a rule to share interaction terms, such as
Brinson's in section 2.1.

</Optional>

### 1.3 Two kinds of indicator

Indicators also differ in the role they play in trading.

**Signals used to take positions.** A forecast enters the trading policy and
may change its trades. If we can rerun the policy, we can remove signals, run
them alone or combine subsets. These runs share one market path and one risk
convention, and what each signal earned depends on both. Section 3 shares the resulting P&L
between signals, and section 4 explains why that differs from sharing
predictive information between indicators.

**State indicators.** Spread, volatility and market order flow describe the
conditions in which gains and losses occur, and the policy need not use them as
inputs. Inventory and the desk's own orders are different: the desk's signals
produce them, so they belong with the policy, and their link with the P&L
partly reflects the signals.

We can first split the P&L in the fill records into spread and inventory P&L
(section 2.5), then relate each part to the state indicators (section 4).

The same variable can play both roles. A volatility forecast fed to the policy
is a signal, and removing it before rerunning the policy changes the trades.
Realised volatility used to compare fills in calm and volatile periods is a
state indicator; the comparison is descriptive.

## 2. The classical answers

Classical methods, which use neither signals nor state indicators, split a
return or P&L against a benchmark, a factor model or a reference price. Each
split adds up by construction and depends on a convention. Sections 2.2 and 2.4
are exceptions: the first fits a benchmark, the second measures how uncertain a
split is.

### 2.1 Allocation, selection, interaction

Brinson attribution compares a manager's portfolio with a benchmark index,
both divided into *segments*. A segment is a group of assets such as a country
(the choice in the paper below), a sector or an asset class.

The manager chooses how much to invest in each segment and which stocks to
hold within it. The benchmark holds each segment's stocks in index
proportions. Within a segment, the portfolio's stocks can earn more or less
than the benchmark's stocks.

The figure below uses a synthetic portfolio with two segments, A and B. The
portfolio holds 60% in A and 40% in B, and the benchmark holds the reverse.
Over the period, the benchmark's stocks returned 600 basis points (bp) in A and
200 bp in B, while the portfolio's stocks returned 800 bp and 100 bp.

<PaperNote id="brinsonMeasuringNonUSEquity1985" look="Eqs. (3)–(7), pp. 75–76">

The interaction term is an exact cross product rather than a residual, and the
choice of comparison portfolios decides whether it is reported alone or inside
selection.

</PaperNote>

@brinsonMeasuringNonUSEquity1985 split the active return of non-US equity
portfolios into three amounts that add up exactly. *Allocation* is the gain
from weighting segments differently from the benchmark. *Selection* is the gain
from better choices within each segment. *Interaction* is the cross term between
the two.

For each segment $i$, write $w_i$ and $b_i$ for its weights in the portfolio
and in the benchmark, $r_i$ for the portfolio's return within the segment and
$r_i^B$ for the segment's benchmark return. Both sets of weights sum to one,
and the total returns are

$$
R_P=\sum_i w_i r_i,
\qquad R_B=\sum_i b_i r_i^B.
$$

In the example, $R_P=0.6\times800+0.4\times100=520$ bp and
$R_B=0.4\times600+0.6\times200=360$ bp, an active return of 160 bp.

The allocation term of Brinson and Fachler (1985) is

$$
A_i=(w_i-b_i)(r_i^B-R_B).
$$

It uses the segment's benchmark return, so choices within the segment are not
yet credited. The selection term is

$$
S_i=b_i(r_i-r_i^B).
$$

Selection is applied at the benchmark's weight in the segment. The interaction
term is

$$
I_i=(w_i-b_i)(r_i-r_i^B).
$$

Interaction applies the same return difference to the over- or underweight.
Together the three terms give

$$
R_P-R_B=\sum_i(A_i+S_i+I_i).
$$

<Optional title="Where the three terms come from" subtitle="add and subtract, then use the fact that the weights sum to one">

We start with the difference of the totals:

$$
R_P-R_B=\sum_i(w_i r_i-b_i r_i^B).
$$

To separate weights from returns, we add and subtract $w_i r_i^B$ and $b_i r_i$ inside each
segment's contribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_i r_i-b_i r_i^B
&=(w_i r_i^B-b_i r_i^B)\\
&\quad +(b_i r_i-b_i r_i^B)\\
&\quad +(w_i r_i-w_i r_i^B\\
&\qquad -b_i r_i+b_i r_i^B).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line changes only the weights, using benchmark segment returns.
The second changes only the segment returns, with benchmark weights.
The last two lines account for simultaneous changes in weights and returns.

The weight differences sum to zero, because an overweight in one segment is
offset by underweights elsewhere. We can therefore subtract the total benchmark
return from each segment return in the first term without changing the sum:

$$
\sum_i(w_i-b_i)R_B=0.
$$

The three lines then become $A_i$, $S_i$ and $I_i$.

</Optional>

For segment A, in basis points,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_A&=(0.6-0.4)(600-360)=48,\\
S_A&=0.4(800-600)=80,\\
I_A&=(0.6-0.4)(800-600)=40.
\end{aligned}
$$

All three terms are positive for A. The portfolio overweights a segment
whose benchmark beats the overall benchmark, and its stocks also beat that
segment benchmark. For B, in basis points,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_B&=(0.4-0.6)(200-360)=32,\\
S_B&=0.6(100-200)=-60,\\
I_B&=(0.4-0.6)(100-200)=20.
\end{aligned}
$$

In B, the underweight in the lower-return segment helps allocation (+32 bp);
selection is negative (−60 bp) because B's investments lag their benchmark, and
interaction is positive (+20 bp) because the underweight reduces that loss.
Across both segments, allocation contributes 80 bp, selection 20 bp and
interaction 60 bp. Their sum is 160 bp.

A year later, @brinsonDeterminantsPortfolioPerformance1986 measure allocation
with the segment benchmark return directly:

$$
A_i^{\mathrm{1986}}=(w_i-b_i)r_i^B.
$$

Compared with the 1985 term, each segment's allocation gains $(w_i-b_i)R_B$.
These amounts sum to zero, so the total allocation is unchanged while the
segment entries move.

A separate choice is to measure selection at portfolio weights:

$$
S_i^{\mathrm{portfolio}}=w_i(r_i-r_i^B)=S_i+I_i.
$$

This folds interaction into selection, so it is not reported separately. Both
conventions account for the full active return, so the choice rests on what
selection should measure: stock picks at benchmark weights, or at the weights
actually held.

inputs fixed. The area view draws one segment's terms as weight times return,
and the bars show both segments and their totals in basis points.

### 2.2 A fitted exposure need not be a holding

Brinson's comparison needs the portfolio's holdings. A returns-based approach
estimates a comparison portfolio from the fund's returns alone.

<PaperNote id="sharpeAssetAllocationManagement1992" look="Table 2, p. 10; Figures 14–17, pp. 17–18">

When indices are correlated, several mixes can fit the returns. In our
reading, a fund that changes its mix during the evaluation period has that
change counted as selection, because the style is fitted on earlier months.

</PaperNote>

@sharpeAssetAllocationManagement1992 finds the long-only mix of asset-class
indices that tracks a fund's returns most closely and calls it the fund's
*style*. The style describes how the fund behaved, which need not be what it
held. Sharpe estimates the style on earlier data and assesses selection on later
data. The benchmark is therefore not fitted to the period it evaluates.

With $y_t$ the fund's return and $r_{k,t}$ the return of index $k$, the style
weights solve

$$
\min_{w}\ \operatorname{Var}\Big(y_t-\sum_k w_k r_{k,t}\Big)
\quad\text{with } w_k\geq0,\ \sum_k w_k=1 .
$$

Only the variance of the difference is minimised, so its mean is left free.

### 2.3 Factor return and factor risk

With recorded positions and specified factor exposures, position P&L can be
divided into factor and specific terms.

A linear factor model writes the vector of asset returns at time $t$ as

$$
r_t=B_t f_t+u_t.
$$

Here $B_t$ holds the assets' factor exposures, $f_t$ the factor returns and
$u_t$ the part of asset returns the factors leave unexplained. Currency positions $h_t$ give the amount held in
each asset over the return interval. Position P&L is

$$
h_t^\top r_t=(B_t^\top h_t)^\top f_t+h_t^\top u_t.
$$

The vector $B_t^\top h_t$ gives the portfolio's factor exposures.
Factor exposure times factor return gives each factor's contribution;
the remaining term is specific P&L.
The same split works with any linear return model: for a portfolio built from
alpha forecasts, each alpha can serve as a factor, with the forecast values as
its exposures.

These terms add up even when factors are correlated. Correlation affects risk
and how the factors are read, but adds no interaction term to this linear
identity. A rotation of the factor basis replaces the factors with linear
combinations of them; it can change individual contributions and leaves the
total unchanged
[@grinoldActivePortfolioManagement2000; @paleologoElementsQuantitativeInvesting2025, p. 71].

Over a short history the mean of each component is uncertain, as the next
figure shows; [section 2.4](#24-estimated-components-are-uncertain) adds a
second uncertainty, from estimating the factor returns.

<WhatToNotice>

Increase exposure to 4: factor shocks then cause most of the day-to-day variance, while the model's expected gain still comes only from the specific signal. Select a short history, then press “New history” to see how realised mean shares change. The sample covariance between factor and specific P&L can be nonzero even though the model sets it to zero.

</WhatToNotice>

Returns split additively; risk does not, because positions co-move. For a
fixed covariance matrix $\Sigma$ and a position vector $x$, portfolio
volatility is

$$
\sigma(x)=\sqrt{x^\top\Sigma x}.
$$

Volatility scales with the portfolio: multiplying every position by a positive
constant multiplies volatility by the same constant. When volatility is positive, Euler's
theorem then splits it into one risk contribution $\mathrm{RC}_i$ per position:

$$
\sigma(x)=\sum_i x_i\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial x_i},
\qquad
\mathrm{RC}_i=\frac{x_i(\Sigma x)_i}{\sigma(x)}.
$$

<PaperNote id="littermanHotSpotsHedges1996" look="Exhibit 13, p. 60; Exhibit 22, p. 66">

The risk triangle draws two position volatilities as sides, with correlation
setting the angle and portfolio volatility the third side.

</PaperNote>

The hot-spots report of @littermanHotSpotsHedges1996 lists these risk
contributions next to the position sizes. Each contribution depends on the
position's covariance with the whole portfolio, not only on its size and
volatility. A hedge can contribute negatively, and the largest position need
not contribute the most risk.

The decomposition allocates volatility, not the realised P&L of a given day.

### 2.4 Estimated components are uncertain

<PaperNote id="paleologoElementsQuantitativeInvesting2025" look="§14.2.2–14.2.3, pp. 337–340">

Gives standard errors for the split between factor and specific P&L.

</PaperNote>

Paleologo shows that factor and specific P&L are uncertain even when their
sum is exact, because the factor returns are themselves estimated. Specific
P&L is position P&L minus factor P&L, so an error in factor P&L appears with
the opposite sign in specific P&L. The errors cancel in the total.

<Optional title="How the uncertainty is computed" subtitle="fixed model, normal returns, weighted least squares">

In this model, factor returns are estimated each period by a cross-sectional
weighted least-squares regression of asset returns on the exposures $B_t$.
Paleologo treats these estimates as noisy and calculates the resulting
uncertainty in factor P&L. His standard errors assume a fixed model and normal
returns, and they do not cover exposures that change intraday.

</Optional>

### 2.5 Spread and inventory: a market maker's P&L

A market maker's P&L is computed from its fill records, which we call the
*ledger*, rather than from position snapshots. With inventory marked at the
final midprice, the P&L before fees splits exactly into the spread earned at
each fill and the gain or loss on the market maker's inventory, called
*positioning*. Net spread is that spread after
fees.

Consider a hypothetical purchase of one share. The midprice before the fill is
€100.00, and the market maker buys at €99.98, so the fill earns €0.02 against
that midprice. After a fee of €0.01, the net spread is €0.01. The midprice then
falls to €99.95: the share loses €0.05 against the initial midprice, and the
marked P&L after fees is €0.01 − €0.05 = −€0.04.

<HtmlEmbed src="w-i5-mm-ledger.html" data="w-i5-mm-ledger.json" title="One hypothetical fill" desc="Net spread plus positioning gives the marked P&amp;L after fees. No rebate, hedge or liquidation cost is included." alt="Hypothetical purchase of one share. Prices share one linear vertical scale against time: midprice before 100.00 euros, fill price 99.98, later midprice 99.95. The first bracket is the spread before fees, 0.02; the second is positioning, −0.05. The formulas subtract the 0.01 fee to give net spread +0.01, positioning −0.05 and P&amp;L after fees −0.04. Hover or focus a bracket or formula to highlight its segment." />

For fill $i$, let $d_i$ be its signed quantity, positive for a purchase, $p_i$
its price, $\rho_i$ a chosen reference price and $m_T$ the final midprice. The
split is

$$
d_i(m_T-p_i)=\underbrace{d_i(\rho_i-p_i)}_{\text{spread}}+\underbrace{d_i(m_T-\rho_i)}_{\text{positioning}}.
$$

As with Brinson, the total is fixed and the split depends on a convention, here
the reference price. A later reference moves money between positioning and
spread without changing the total. A fill's *markout* is its spread measured
against the midprice a set time $\tau$ after the fill.

<PaperNote id="menkveldHighFrequencyTrading2013" look="Tables 2–3, pp. 33–34, TI 11-076/2/DSF 21 (2011)">

Page numbers follow the 2011 discussion paper, not the 2013 journal version.

</PaperNote>

@menkveldHighFrequencyTrading2013 [pp. 16, 33] applies this split
to one high-frequency market maker, with the midprice just before each fill as
reference.
The trader earned €1.55 per trade in net spread and lost €0.68 per trade on
positioning.

<Optional title="Menkveld's sample, the full P&L and markouts" subtitle="data, hedges and fees, later references, paper portfolio">

**Sample.** @menkveldHighFrequencyTrading2013 [pp. 11, 28] examines one unnamed high-frequency trader on NYSE Euronext and Chi-X, in 14 Dutch index stocks over 200 trading days, from 4 September 2007 to 17 June 2008. The trader made 1,397 trades per stock per day, an index-weighted average across stocks (Table 1, p. 32). Table 2 reports gross profit of €0.88 per trade; the net spread minus the positioning loss gives €0.87, and the difference of one cent comes from rounding in the table. Gross profit here excludes fixed costs (footnote 9, p. 12).

A frequency decomposition divides inventory into components that vary over different horizons, and Menkveld applies it to positioning profit. For this trader and sample, in the all-stocks column, the component at horizons up to five seconds earns positive positioning profit; every longer horizon band loses (Table 3, p. 34). Menkveld labels these bands position durations (p. 29), and we read them as periods of inventory cycles rather than holding times of individual positions.

**The full P&L.** Assume that the market maker starts with zero inventory and marks the terminal inventory at $m_T$, without liquidation costs. Summing over fills,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Spr}(\rho)&=\sum_i d_i(\rho_i-p_i),\\
\mathrm{Pos}(\rho)&=\sum_i d_i(m_T-\rho_i),
\end{aligned}
$$

and the P&L is

$$
\Pi=\mathrm{Spr}(\rho)+\mathrm{Pos}(\rho)+H-\mathrm{fees}+\mathrm{rebates}.
$$

Here $H$ is the gross marked P&L of a separately booked hedge instrument. Fees and rebates are separate cash lines covering both quoting and hedging. The sum runs over quoting fills only. If the fill records also hold the hedge trades, their P&L is already in $\mathrm{Spr}(\rho)$ and $\mathrm{Pos}(\rho)$, and $H$ is dropped. With the midprice just before each fill as $\rho_i$, $\mathrm{Spr}(\rho)$ minus the fills' fees plus their rebates gives Menkveld's net spread, and $\mathrm{Pos}(\rho)$ gives his positioning profit.

**Later references.** Moving the reference from the midprice before the fill to a later midprice adds $d_i(\rho_i^{\text{later}}-\rho_i^{\text{pre}})$ to spread and subtracts the same amount from positioning. In the quoting model of @gueantDealingInventoryRisk2013, which includes adverse price jumps at fills, the jump enters positioning when the reference is the midprice before the jump; with a later reference, part of the loss enters measured spread. A later reference also includes unrelated price moves and subsequent trades, so the transfer does not estimate the current fill's adverse selection.

**Markouts.** For a fill at time $t_i$, let $m(t_i+\tau)$ be the midprice $\tau$ seconds later. Its signed execution-to-midprice markout is $d_i[m(t_i+\tau)-p_i]$, which is $\mathrm{Spr}(\rho)$ for that fill with $\rho_i=m(t_i+\tau)$. Averaged over fills and plotted against $\tau$, it gives the markout curve. When fill sizes differ, the amount per unit traded (dividing by $\sum_i \lvert d_i\rvert$) is easier to compare; we report it with the fill count and traded quantity.

**Paper portfolio.** @peroldImplementationShortfallPaper1988 defines implementation shortfall against a paper portfolio fixed at decision time, and compares that paper portfolio with the portfolio actually implemented. A market maker revises its quotes continuously and executes no fixed list of intended trades, so its paper portfolio is not defined; the ledger accounts for fills and does not value missed opportunities.

</Optional>

conditions such as the quoted spread, volatility or market order flow, with the
methods of section 4.

## 3. Attributing a P&L to signals

Factor attribution divides the P&L of positions already held. Signal attribution also needs to account for the policy that produced those positions.

A portfolio receives $K$ forecasts, the signals, but holds a single position.
The portfolio construction combines the forecasts with risk, transaction costs
and position limits. A signal that asks for a purchase may reinforce another
signal, offset it, or leave the portfolio unchanged. To attribute the
resulting P&L, we need to know how the signals become trades.

A policy that we can rerun lets us compare its outputs with different signals
as inputs. Known optimality equations let us split the joint solution directly.
@grinoldAttributionPerformanceHoldings1998 do this for an optimised portfolio,
with one portfolio per signal and one per binding constraint. A constraint
binds when its limit holds with equality at the optimum. Durin's variant in
section 3.3 spreads the constraints over the signals. Coalition methods need
many more reruns but no optimality equations. Because the methods use different
inputs, a signal's contribution means something different in each.

### 3.1 Stand-alone portfolios as proxies

A common method reruns the policy once per signal, with only that signal
switched on. It then fits the total P&L with a weighted mix of these
stand-alone P&Ls, by nonnegative least squares. Each signal's contribution is
its weighted stand-alone P&L, and what the mix cannot reproduce stays in a
residual.

Each run keeps the construction rules, including costs and constraints, and
gives the signal its own risk target: a chosen level of predicted portfolio
volatility. The stand-alone P&L therefore includes the trades the signal
generates: a fast signal's turnover, a slow signal's carried positions. The
method fits these P&Ls rather than the raw forecasts.

<Optional title="The proxy regression" subtitle="nonnegative least squares without intercept, residual">

Let $y_t$ be the total P&L in period $t$ and $\pi_{k,t}(\sigma_k^\star)$ the
proxy for signal $k$ at risk target $\sigma_k^\star$. We fit nonnegative least
squares (NNLS) without an intercept:

$$
\widehat\beta
=\arg\min_{\beta_k\geq0}
\sum_t\left(y_t-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k \pi_{k,t}(\sigma_k^\star)\right)^2.
$$

Writing $\pi_{k,t}$ for $\pi_{k,t}(\sigma_k^\star)$, signal $k$ is assigned
$\widehat\beta_k \pi_{k,t}$, and the residual is kept as a separate term:

$$
\varepsilon_t=y_t-\sum_k\widehat\beta_k \pi_{k,t}.
$$

</Optional>

The fit has no constant term, so it cannot absorb an average gain or loss, and
its nonnegative coefficients cannot reverse a proxy's sign. A losing proxy
receives a negative contribution if its coefficient is positive, and none if
the fit sets it to zero. Because the signals are correlated, trades offset and
constraints bind, the coefficients are not independent risk budgets in the
combined portfolio.

The one-step regression stops there. The iterated method rescales each
signal's risk target by its fitted coefficient, reruns the stand-alone
portfolios and fits again.

<Optional title="Iterating on the risk targets" subtitle="undamped update and fixed point">

In the simulation below, the optimiser adjusts risk aversion each period to
reach each run's risk target when the constraints permit it. The update is

$$
\sigma_k^\star\leftarrow\widehat\beta_k\sigma_k^\star.
$$

The update is undamped: each target is multiplied by the full fitted
coefficient. At a fixed point every surviving proxy has coefficient one, and a
proxy whose coefficient reaches zero drops out.

A scaled proxy can differ from the proxy of a new run at the new risk target, because the construction is nonlinear. The method therefore repeats the regression.

</Optional>

@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014 reports regressions on signal
portfolios as existing practice (pp. 2–3). He rejects them as a general method,
because binding constraints can leave a large residual. Section 3.3 describes
his alternative.

The residual collects the P&L of joint trades that no fixed nonnegative mix of
stand-alone P&Ls matches. Assigning it to the signals would make the
contributions add up without explaining more of the P&L.

A small residual means only that the proxies fit the total in this sample, not
that they are causal or that they predict what removing a signal would do.
When proxies are similar, their coefficients need not be unique.

### 3.2 Removing one signal from the full portfolio

Leave-one-out attribution asks what the portfolio loses when one signal is
removed. It runs the construction with all signals, then once with each signal
left out. @isichenkoQuantitativePortfolioManagement2021 [p. 134] scores a
forecast in this way, by how much the backtest deteriorates without it.

The leave-one-out contribution of a signal is the P&L of the full run minus the
P&L of the run without that signal, with both runs under the same risk
convention. A signal that is redundant with another can have a substantial
stand-alone P&L and a small leave-one-out contribution. A signal that helps the
combined forecast clear a trading threshold (section 3.3) can have the reverse
pattern.

Neither method guarantees that the contributions sum to the total, as Isichenko
notes for leave-one-out. When several signals jointly enable a trade, removing
any one of them removes the same gain, so the sum of removal effects counts
that gain more than once.

Both methods keep the market path, the cost model and the initial positions
fixed across runs. Only the signals and their risk targets change, and we state
which risk convention each run uses. The replay also assumes that removing a
signal leaves market prices unchanged. The remaining trades fill at observed
prices or through a fill model for passive orders, and the desk's own impact is
left out.

### 3.3 Using the optimiser's internal equations

@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014 uses the optimiser's own equations
to split its actual position into one position per signal, and the pieces add
up to that position. Each signal receives the P&L of its piece and a share of
the joint risk and cost. This works because, with the binding constraints
fixed, the optimal trade is affine in the forecasts. The method therefore needs
a known convex optimiser with a quadratic risk or cost term. It reads that
optimiser's positions, binding constraints and Lagrange multipliers.

<Optional title="What the split assumes: an affine map and frozen multipliers" subtitle="active set, intercepts, frozen multipliers">

With the set of binding constraints fixed, the optimality conditions are
linear equations in the trade and the multipliers. The forecasts and the
bounds sit on the right-hand side. The optimal trade is therefore affine in
the forecasts: a bound with a nonzero value, such as a position bound $M_c$,
contributes an intercept that does not scale with any forecast.

Durin's split has no separate intercept because the multipliers, evaluated at
the joint optimum, enter the shared matrices of the derivation below. With these
matrices held fixed, the recursion is linear in each
signal's forecast. Holding them fixed is a convention: the signal positions add
up to the joint position, but a signal's position is not the position the
optimiser would hold for that signal alone.

The inverses below require the matrix $Q$ and its constrained version
$\bar Q_t$, defined in the derivation, to be nonsingular on the free subspace:
the directions in which the binding constraints still let the trade move.

</Optional>

<Limits>

- **No optimality equations.** Heuristic position rules, black-box models and learned policies do not supply these equations. Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.4 describe rerun methods, and section 5.3 returns to such opaque policies.
- **Dynamic model.** @durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014 solves for the next trade only and covers the dynamic model of @garleanuDynamicTradingPredictable2013 through modified forecasts and matrices (pp. 4–5); constraining only the next trade while keeping the unconstrained value function approximates the full constrained dynamic program (p. 7).
- **Excluded constraints.** Combinatorial and non-convex constraints, such as round lots or a minimum trade size, are outside this treatment.
- **Linear and mixed-integer programs.** With no quadratic cost or risk term, a linear program's optimum sits at a vertex of the feasible set and is piecewise constant in the forecasts, so it has no linear split. Mixed-integer problems that carry state between periods, such as on/off decisions with start-up costs, are excluded too.

</Limits>

<Optional title="Derivation: one position per signal" subtitle="objective, recursion, P&L, risk and cost split, constraints, zero bounds">

Equation and page numbers in this block refer to
@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014.

Let $x_{t-1}$ be the previous position and $\Delta x_t$ the trade. Let $g_t^k$
be the expected return supplied by signal $k$ for the next period. The
combined forecast is $G_t=\sum_k g_t^k$. This sum assumes additive alpha
signals. For the features of a fitted model, removing a feature can mean
refitting, marginalising or imputing, and each gives a different attribution
[@covertExplainingRemovingUnified2021]. After the trade the position is
$x_t=x_{t-1}+\Delta x_t$. In the static model, the optimiser chooses the
trade that maximises the following objective. It subtracts a risk penalty and
a quadratic trading cost from the new position's expected return:

$$
x_t^\top G_t-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\,x_t^\top\Sigma x_t-\tfrac12\,\Delta x_t^\top\Lambda\Delta x_t .
$$

Here $\Sigma$ is the covariance of returns, $\gamma$ the risk aversion and
$\Lambda$ the matrix of quadratic trading costs. We replace $x_t$ by
$x_{t-1}+\Delta x_t$ and drop the terms that do not depend on the trade. What
remains is

$$
\begin{gathered}
\Delta x_t^\top G_t-\tfrac12\,\Delta x_t^\top Q\Delta x_t-\Delta x_t^\top Px_{t-1},\\
Q=\Lambda+\gamma\Sigma,\quad P=\gamma\Sigma .
\end{gathered}
$$

The matrix $Q$ combines the cost of trading and the risk the trade adds. The
matrix $P$ measures how the trade interacts with the risk of the previous
position. Durin writes the objective in this form (Eq. 5, p. 4), with the
static matrices of Eq. 7 (p. 5). The same form holds in the dynamic model of
Gârleanu and Pedersen, on which his paper builds, with different matrices $Q$
and $P$ and forecasts adjusted for each signal's decay rate.

Setting the derivative with respect to $\Delta x_t$ to zero gives the
optimality equation (Eq. 8, p. 6)

$$
Q\Delta x_t+Px_{t-1}=\sum_k g_t^k.
$$

The equation is linear in the forecasts and the previous position. If the
portfolio starts flat, as in the simulation of section 3.5, each signal starts
from $x_0^k=0$. A nonzero initial position belongs to no signal; one choice
carries it as one more component with zero forecast, which the recursion
unwinds. At each step we compute each signal's trade, then update its position
(Eqs. 11–12, p. 6):

$$
\Delta x_t^k=Q^{-1}(g_t^k-Px_{t-1}^k).
$$

The trade responds to this signal's forecast and the position it already
holds.

$$
x_t^k=x_{t-1}^k+\Delta x_t^k,
\qquad x_t=\sum_k x_t^k.
$$

The component positions sum to the actual position (Eq. 9, p. 6). The inverses
require nonsingular matrices (see the affine-map block); zero bounds come last.

Let $r_{t+1}$ be the vector of realised returns over the next holding period.
The gross P&L assigned to signal $k$ is

$$
\Pi_{t+1}^k=(x_t^k)^\top r_{t+1}.
$$

Multiplying every component by the same returns gives an exact gross P&L split,
as Durin notes after Eq. 12 (p. 6). For risk, write $V$ for portfolio variance
and $V_k$ for the share assigned to signal $k$ (Eq. 13, p. 6):

$$
V=x_t^\top\Sigma x_t
=\sum_k (x_t^k)^\top\Sigma x_t
=\sum_k V_k.
$$

Each share measures the component's covariance with the full portfolio,
including its interaction with the other signals. For quadratic cost $C$, the
analogous allocation is (Eq. 14, p. 7)

$$
\begin{aligned}
C&=\tfrac12\Delta x_t^\top\Lambda\Delta x_t=\sum_k C_k,\\
C_k&=\tfrac12(\Delta x_t^k)^\top\Lambda\Delta x_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

A component receives its share of the cost of the joint trade, including the
savings when component trades offset each other.

**Two ways to handle constraints.** @grinoldAttributionPerformanceHoldings1998
treat each binding constraint as one more source of P&L, next to the signals,
as Durin describes (pp. 2–3). In Durin's notation, the optimality equation
becomes (Eq. 17, p. 7)

$$
Q\Delta x_t+Px_{t-1}=G_t+\sum_c\theta_{t,c}.
$$

Here $c$ indexes constraints. The vector $\theta_{t,c}$ is the signed
contribution of a constraint's multiplier to the equation, including its
exposure direction. Running the same recursion on each source gives a
constraint portfolio beside the signal portfolios, hence a P&L per constraint.
It does not say which signals should bear that constraint's effect (pp. 7–8).

Durin instead folds each binding constraint into the risk and cost matrices
that every signal uses. A binding position constraint contributes extra
quadratic risk at the optimum; a binding trade constraint contributes extra
quadratic cost. Write $\bar Q_t$ and $\bar P_t$ for the resulting matrices.
Durin's signal-wise recursion (Eq. 24, p. 10, which reuses the recursion of
Eqs. 10–12, p. 6) is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x_t^k&=\bar Q_t^{-1}(g_t^k-\bar P_t x_{t-1}^k),\\
x_t^k&=x_{t-1}^k+\Delta x_t^k.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each signal's trades therefore include the effect of the binding constraints.
A binding trade limit slows trading in the
constrained direction. A position limit shrinks the aim portfolio (the target the trade moves towards)
in that direction, but can make the adjustment towards it faster. In Durin's value/momentum example, the no-short constraint affects even the signal that points away from the forbidden short position. The adjustment depends on each signal's position
from previous trades (Eq. 34, p. 13).

**From active bounds to effective matrices.** Let $a_c$ be the vector defining an exposure and $M_c$ its active bound. For a
sector exposure, $a_c$ has ones on the sector's stocks and zeros elsewhere.

At an active upper position bound, $a_c^\top x_t=M_c$. Durin uses the squared
constraint $(a_c^\top x_t)^2\leq M_c^2$ and a multiplier $\eta_c$ for it
(Eqs. 26–29, p. 11). A lower bound is treated in the same way when it is the
active one.

At the active bound, the squared constraint gives the same optimality equation.
Squaring a one-sided bound can change its feasible set, so the
nonnegative-penalty reading holds for bounds that reduce the trade or position,
in particular when zero is admissible (p. 14).

We remove the terms that are constant in the trade. For one position constraint,
the augmented objective is (Eq. 29, p. 11)

$$
\begin{aligned}
F={}&-\tfrac12\Delta x_t^\top Q\Delta x_t
      -\Delta x_t^\top Px_{t-1}\\
   &+\Delta x_t^\top G_t
      -\eta_c x_t^\top(a_c\otimes a_c)x_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last term penalises squared exposure. The matrix
$a_c\otimes a_c=a_ca_c^\top$ is an outer product. With $A_c=2a_c\otimes a_c$,
differentiation gives (Eqs. 29–31, pp. 11–12)

$$
\begin{gathered}
(Q+\eta_c A_c)\Delta x_t\\
=G_t-(P+\eta_c A_c)x_{t-1}.
\end{gathered}
$$

One position constraint adds $\eta_c A_c$ to both matrices. In the static
model, it adds $\eta_c A_c/\gamma$ to the covariance matrix. A squared trade
constraint, with multiplier $\mu_c$, adds
$-\mu_c\Delta x_t^\top(a_c\otimes a_c)\Delta x_t$ to the objective and changes
only $Q$ (p. 12).

For several constraints, let $\mathcal T$ index trade constraints and
$\mathcal P$ position constraints. Including the time dependence of the
multipliers, Eq. 31 (p. 12) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar Q_t&=Q+\sum_{c\in\mathcal T}\mu_{t,c}A_c\\
        &\quad+\sum_{c\in\mathcal P}\eta_{t,c}A_c,\\
\bar P_t&=P+\sum_{c\in\mathcal P}\eta_{t,c}A_c.
\end{aligned}
$$

Only position constraints change the term involving the previous position.
Inactive constraints have zero multipliers. For individual stock bounds, these
additions are diagonal, as in Durin's long-only example (Eq. 32, p. 12).

The solver supplies multipliers for the original linear bounds. Denote one
such scalar by $\nu_c$. Unlike the vector $\theta_{t,c}$ of Eq. 17, it
excludes the exposure vector. Let $s$ equal $+1$ for an
upper bound and $-1$ for a lower bound in the maximisation convention. The
attribution multiplier is (Eqs. 35–37, p. 14)

$$
\eta_c=\frac{s\,\nu_c}{2M_c}.
$$

This converts sensitivity to the original bound into sensitivity to its
square. The same conversion gives $\mu_c$ for a trade bound.

At a zero bound this division cannot be used directly. For one trade
constraint, define the adjusted forecast $\tilde g_t^k=g_t^k-Px_{t-1}^k$ and
let $a$ be its exposure vector. Applying Durin's rank-one inverse to each
signal gives (Eqs. 39–41, p. 15)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x_t^k
&=\left[Q^{-1}
 -\alpha\frac{Q^{-1}aa^\top Q^{-1}}{a^\top Q^{-1}a}\right]\tilde g_t^k,\\
\alpha&=\frac{2\mu a^\top Q^{-1}a}
                 {1+2\mu a^\top Q^{-1}a}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As the active bound tends to zero and $\mu$ tends to infinity, $\alpha$ tends
to one. This projects the trade onto $a^\top\Delta x_t^k=0$, along $Q^{-1}a$
(Eq. 42, pp. 15–16). For a position bound, we use its multiplier $\eta$ in
$\alpha$; the trade expression has the additional term (Eqs. 43–44, p. 16)

$$
-\alpha\frac{Q^{-1}aa^\top}{a^\top Q^{-1}a}\,x_{t-1}^k.
$$

It removes the previous position's exposure too, so the limiting component
position satisfies $a^\top x_t^k=0$. The infinite multiplier therefore has a
finite, well-defined effect on the attributed trades and positions.

**Spread costs and the no-trade band.** The portfolio trades only when the
combined forecast is large enough to cover the spread. For smaller forecasts it
stays inside a no-trade band, and no signal trades. Two signals can cancel
inside the band, or push the combined forecast out of it together. Each signal
pays the spread on its part of the joint trade and is credited when its part
goes against that trade. Durin reads this sharing of the spread as a voting
mechanism among the signals (p. 18).

<Optional title="Derivation: spread costs and their allocation" subtitle="effective cost, band condition, several assets, turnover and financing">

Equation and page numbers in this block also refer to Durin. He treats the
spread as an extra quadratic cost that grows as the trade gets smaller, and
gives every signal the same extra cost. We take a single asset in the static
model, with scalar return variance $\Sigma$ and scalar quadratic cost
coefficient $\Lambda$ (Durin's $\lambda$ in Eqs. 49–60). A positive spread
$\lambda_0$ is charged per unit traded, adding $-\lambda_0|\Delta x_t|$ to the
objective. For a nonzero joint trade, the effective quadratic-cost multiplier,
which Durin writes $\eta_t$, is (Eqs. 47–48, pp. 16–17)

$$
\eta_t^{\mathrm{spr}}=\frac{\lambda_0}{2|\Delta x_t|}.
$$

Smaller joint trades therefore face a larger effective quadratic cost. The
optimality equation becomes (Eq. 52, p. 17)

$$
(\gamma\Sigma+\Lambda+2\eta_t^{\mathrm{spr}})\Delta x_t
=G_t-\gamma\Sigma x_{t-1}.
$$

Spread costs enter the trade coefficient, leaving the previous-position
coefficient unchanged. Each signal uses the same $\eta_t^{\mathrm{spr}}$, with
$g_t^k$ and $x_{t-1}^k$ on the right. A nonzero trade exists only when
(Eqs. 50–54, p. 17)

$$
|G_t-\gamma\Sigma x_{t-1}|>\lambda_0.
$$

Inside this band, including its boundary, the combined forecast does not
justify paying the spread. The limiting effective cost is infinite, and every
signal's attributed trade is zero.

Stand-alone portfolios face their own thresholds and histories. A regression
coefficient therefore cannot, in general, line up their trading dates with
those of the joint portfolio.

For several assets, spread cost is assigned using the direction of each joint
trade. With $i$ indexing assets, its allocation to signal $k$, a positive cost
to subtract, is (Eq. 22, p. 9)

$$
C_{\mathrm{spread},t}^k
=\lambda_0\sum_i\Delta x_{t,i}^k\,
  \operatorname{sgn}(\Delta x_{t,i}).
$$

Here $\operatorname{sgn}$ is the sign function, with value zero at zero. A
component trading against the full trade can receive a negative cost
allocation because it reduces the net trade to execute. Subtracting these
spread allocations and the quadratic costs from each component's gross P&L
gives an exact net split. The effective costs shape the component trades. They
are not extra cash costs to subtract.

The same construction handles a turnover constraint, with its multiplier in
place of the spread coefficient. Financing costs and leverage constraints
penalise absolute positions instead of absolute trades. They become effective
risk terms and can force every component's position to zero (Durin, Sections
2.3 and 3.4; pp. 8–9 and 18, Eqs. 55–60).

### 3.4 Sharing value across coalitions

Coalition methods rerun the construction on every subset of signals, not only
single signals and single removals. The value of a coalition is the P&L of its
run. The Shapley value from cooperative game theory divides the gain of the
full set over the empty coalition among the signals.

The empty coalition, with no signal, needs a reference policy. The Shapley
contributions add up to the full P&L minus the P&L of that policy, and they
depend on it, as SHAP shares depend on their baseline in section 4.2. Below,
the reference policy does not trade, so the empty coalition earns nothing.

The Shapley value credits a signal with its marginal contribution, the extra
P&L when it joins a coalition. It averages that contribution over all orders in
which the $K$ signals can enter. A signal's stand-alone value is then its
marginal contribution when it enters first. Its leave-one-out contribution is
its marginal contribution when it enters last, and Shapley also uses every
coalition in between.

<Optional title="The Shapley formula and the empty coalition" subtitle="marginal contributions, weights, reference policy, risk convention">

Let $N$ be the set of the $K$ signals, so $K=|N|$. The value $v(S)$ of a
coalition $S\subseteq N$ is the P&L of the construction with only the signals
in $S$, under a given risk convention. The stand-alone value of signal $k$ is
$v(\{k\})$, and its leave-one-out contribution is

$$
L_k=v(N)-v(N\setminus\{k\}).
$$

The stand-alone proxy of section 3.1 equals $v(\{k\})$ only under the same risk
convention, so updating the proxies' risk targets defines a different game.

The value $v(\varnothing)$ is the P&L of the reference policy, zero here. With
a reference policy that trades, $v(S)$ is replaced by $v(S)-v(\varnothing)$.

The marginal contribution of signal $k$ to a coalition $S$ is

$$
\Delta_k v(S)=v(S\cup\{k\})-v(S).
$$

This is the extra P&L when signal $k$ joins the signals already in $S$. The
contribution of signal $k$ is

$$
\phi_k=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{k\}}
\omega(S)\,\Delta_k v(S),
\qquad
\omega(S)=\frac{|S|!\,(K-|S|-1)!}{K!}.
$$

The Shapley weight $\omega(S)$ is the probability that, in a uniformly random
order of the $K$ signals, exactly the signals of $S$ come before $k$: there are
$|S|!\,(K-|S|-1)!$ such orders out of $K!$. For each order, the successive
increments sum to $v(N)-v(\varnothing)$, so their average does too:
$\sum_k\phi_k=v(N)-v(\varnothing)$.

</Optional>

<PaperNote id="shapleyValueNpersonGames1953" look="RM-670 (1951), Section 5 (bargaining procedure), pp. 12–13">

@shapleyValueNpersonGames1953 defines the value by a random-order procedure:
each player receives its average contribution on joining the players already
present. Applied here, it divides the P&L of a coalition game with given
trading and risk conventions. Page numbers refer to the 1951
RAND memorandum RM-670, *Notes on the n-person game, II*. The 1953 book chapter
is paginated differently.

</PaperNote>

In an additive game the stand-alone values already sum to the total, and
Shapley returns them. Otherwise the gap between a signal's Shapley contribution
and its stand-alone value is the part of the interaction that Shapley gives to
that signal. The proxy regression, by contrast, fits only the stand-alone
series.

Exact Shapley needs a full backtest of the optimiser for every nonempty
coalition, and the number of coalitions doubles with each added signal. With
many signals, sampling random orders of entry can reduce the number of
backtests. Grouping signals into families, as in Owen's value, also cuts the
cost but gives an allocation different from Shapley's. Durin's
[internal allocation](#33-using-the-optimisers-internal-equations), by
contrast, needs no rerun at all.

On our synthetic histories with up to eight signals, no sampler reached the
target precision (defined below) with fewer coalition values than exact
enumeration. Owen's grouped value needed fewer values and stayed close to
Shapley, while the approximation that drops interactions above order two
performed poorly. Up to eight signals, exact enumeration was the cheapest way
to compute Shapley's allocation itself. Owen's value costs less but gives a
different allocation. With more signals, sampling becomes necessary, and its
error is worth comparing with the dispersion across histories.

weighted regression on sampled coalition values.
@covertImprovingKernelSHAPPractical2021 improve this estimator by pairing each
sampled coalition with its complement.

We measure the distance between two share vectors as half the sum of absolute
differences between shares (the half-L1 distance), using the full portfolio's
P&L as the common denominator. History dispersion is the median pairwise
distance between exact share vectors from five histories. It describes the
dispersion across histories and is not a confidence interval.

Sampling error is measured over repeated draws on each fixed history.
@malekiBoundingEstimationError2013 bound the error of sampled Shapley estimates.
Those bounds cover sampling error only, not uncertainty about the trading
history.

</Optional>

<Optional title="The enumeration and sampling study in numbers" subtitle="coalition values needed, target precision, Owen, pairwise approximation" id="approximate-shapley-numbers">

values. Neither reverse-order pairs nor paired KernelSHAP attained the target

distance, and required the worst history's 95th-percentile sampling error to be

The approximation that removes interactions above order two is exact with fixed

## 4. Correlated indicators and the value of a subset

When indicators move together, there are several ways to split credit between them, and each way answers a different question.

State indicators, the second kind of indicator in [section 1.3](#13-two-kinds-of-indicator), are spread, volatility and market order flow; inventory and the desk's own orders belong with the policy. Section 3 shares a P&L between signals by rerunning the policy or through its optimality equations. The policy may ignore state indicators, or we may be unable to rerun it; in either case a fitted model links the indicators to an outcome, such as the daily P&L or the markout of a fill. Attribution then splits what the correlated indicators tell us about that outcome.

- **Value of a subset.** The value $v(S)$ measures what the indicators in a subset $S$ tell us about the outcome. The rule that gives $v(S)$ for every subset is a *game*. The value with no indicator, $v(\varnothing)$, is the baseline.
- **Shapley share.** The Shapley share of an indicator is its gain in $v$ when it joins a subset, averaged over all orders of entry. With $N$ the set of all indicators, the shares sum to $v(N)-v(\varnothing)$.

<Optional title="Other terms used in this section" subtitle="outcome, indicator, model, coalition, null comparison">

- **Outcome.** The quantity to explain, $Y$, such as the daily P&L or the markout of a fill (section 2.5).
- **Indicator.** A recorded variable that may carry information about the outcome, such as volatility or spread. $X_S$ denotes the indicators in a subset $S$, and $x$ their values on one day.
- **Model and prediction.** A model maps the values $x$ of the indicators to a prediction $\hat y(x)$ of the outcome. We fit the model to past data.
- **Subset.** In game language, a subset $S$ of the indicators is a *coalition*.
- **Null comparison.** The same calculation after the outcome is shuffled, so that no indicator carries information about it. It shows how large a share can be by chance (section 4.3).

</Optional>

<HtmlEmbed src="w-i4-shared-information.html" data="w-i4-shared-information.json" title="Correlated indicators share information; the game sets what a share means" alt="Venn diagram of an outcome and two overlapping indicators. Shapley averages each indicator's gain over all orders of entry. The game sets the units, baseline and meaning of the shares, which sum to v(N) − v(∅)." />

### 4.1 Shared information is not a regression coefficient

An indicator correlated with the real driver of the P&L predicts the same gains
and losses without causing them.

When two indicators are near-copies, a regression predicts their joint effect
well but cannot tell them apart. A small change in the sample can move a large
coefficient from one indicator to the other, or reverse its sign. A coefficient
describes a change in one input with the others fixed, and near-copies rarely
move apart in the data; its size also depends on the input's units.

<PaperNote id="lipovetskyAnalysisRegressionGame2001" look="Table II, p. 329">

In the paper's example, the coefficients vary more across bootstrap samples
than the Shapley shares of explained variance. The paper does not claim this in
general.

</PaperNote>

@lipovetskyAnalysisRegressionGame2001 split a regression's explained variance
between the predictors with Shapley's rule. They fit a separate regression to
each subset of predictors. The value of a subset is the fraction of the
outcome's variance that its regression fits:

$$
v(S)=R^2\ \text{of the regression of } Y \text{ on } X_S .
$$

Near-copies share the variance that they explain together. Averaged over the
orders of entry, a redundant indicator gets credit when it enters before its substitute, even if it
adds little when it enters last.

Move the slider: as the two indicators become more similar, the regression's
coefficients swing from one data set to the next, while Shapley keeps giving
each about half.

<HtmlEmbed src="d3-f7-correlated.html" data="f7-correlated.json" title="Two correlated indicators: the regression cannot separate them; Shapley splits them evenly" desc="The figure uses 192 simulated data sets, each with 768 observations of eight indicators, and follows the first two, A and B, whose true coefficients are both 1. The slider sets the correlation between A and B, and within two other pairs of indicators; the last two indicators are independent." />

<WhatToNotice>

When the two indicators are very similar, A's coefficient runs from about −0.9
to 2.5 across the data sets. In 11 of them, one of the two coefficients comes
out negative. Their sum stays near 2: the data fix what the pair does together,
not how to divide it between them.

Shapley gives A between 48% and 51% of what the pair explains. It averages A's
contribution over every order in which the indicators can enter the
regression, and A and B play symmetric roles
[@lipovetskyAnalysisRegressionGame2001]. When the indicators are unrelated,
each one's part depends on its own fit and varies more.

</WhatToNotice>

### 4.2 The game comes before the allocation

Shapley's rule stays the same in every game. The game changes what a subset is
worth, and so what the shares mean. With $K$ indicators, the share of indicator
$k$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_k(v)&=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{k\}}\omega(S)\,\Delta_k v(S),\\
\omega(S)&=\frac{|S|!\,(K-|S|-1)!}{K!},\\
\Delta_k v(S)&=v(S\cup\{k\})-v(S).
\end{aligned}
$$

The weights average the gain of an indicator over all orders of entry. Each
game below answers one question. In the formulas, $\hat y$ is the fitted
model's prediction.

<PaperNote id="grompingEstimatorsRelativeImportance2007">

The same Shapley split of $R^2$ predates its game-theoretic interpretation: it
is the LMG measure of relative importance, named after Lindeman, Merenda and
Gold. Grömping compares it with other variance decompositions and gives its
properties for correlated regressors.

</PaperNote>

**Refitted regression $R^2$.** How much of the day-to-day variation of the P&L
can a regression on these indicators fit?

$$
v(S)=R^2\ \text{of the regression of } Y \text{ on } X_S,\ \text{refitted for each } S .
$$

Training $R^2$ cannot fall when an ordinary least-squares model with an
intercept gains a predictor. Its Shapley shares are therefore nonnegative, even
for predictors that contain only noise.

**Conditional variance.** The fitted model stays fixed. If we know only these
indicators, how much does its predicted P&L vary?

$$
v(S)=\operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat y(X)\mid X_S]\big).
$$

<Optional title="Conditional variance for independent inputs" subtitle="ANOVA and Sobol' bounds">

@owenSobolIndicesShapley2014 studies this game for independent inputs and
derives its Shapley share from the model's variance decomposition (ANOVA). The
share lies between the variance an input explains alone and the variance it
adds when it comes last (two Sobol' indices). With correlated indicators,
conditional variance is still a possible game, but this formula and these
bounds need not hold.

</Optional>

**Local SHAP.** On one day, how far does each indicator move the model's
prediction from its average? Two games answer this question, and they differ
when indicators are correlated. Write $\bar S$ for the indicators not in $S$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{\rm cond}(S)&=\mathbb{E}[\hat y(X)\mid X_S=x_S],\\
v_{\rm int}(S)&=\mathbb{E}\big[\hat y(x_S,X_{\bar S})\big]\quad\text{for one day } x .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the conditional game, the missing indicators are averaged given the known
ones. In the interventional (marginal) game, they are drawn from a background
sample, independently of the known ones.

The interventional game explains what the model computes. The conditional game also credits what an indicator reveals about the indicators
correlated with it. Neither game measures what the P&L would do if an indicator
changed. SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) shares are signed and in the units of the model's output, for
example a predicted P&L in euros [@lundbergUnifiedApproachInterpreting2017].

<Optional title="Conditional or interventional SHAP" subtitle="units, correlated indicators, unused inputs, estimators">

The shares add up to that day's prediction minus the average prediction over
the background set, not to a fraction of variance. They change with the choice
of background set, and an indicator can push one prediction down and another
up. TreeSHAP in its interventional mode and KernelSHAP with a
background set compute the interventional game.

The two games treat a correlated indicator that the model does not use in
different ways. The interventional game gives it zero, because changing it
does not change the prediction. The conditional game spreads credit to it,
because knowing it tells us about the indicators the model does use
[@sundararajanManyShapleyValues2020].
@janzingFeatureRelevanceQuantification2020 argue that explaining a model's
output means intervening on its inputs, which leads to the interventional game.
@chenTrueModelTrue2020 call the
interventional game true to the model and the conditional game true to the
data, and leave the choice to the application.

</Optional>

**SAGE** (Shapley additive global importance). How much smaller is the model's prediction error when it can use these
indicators?

$$
v(S)=\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-\mathbb{E}\,\hat y(X))^2\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[(Y-\mathbb{E}[\hat y(X)\mid X_S])^2\big].
$$

@covertUnderstandingGlobalFeature2020 introduce SAGE. Its value is the reduction
in expected prediction loss relative to using no indicator. Here the loss is
squared error, and the value depends on both the fitted model and the loss.

<Optional title="SAGE in practice, and when the global games agree" subtitle="estimator, population and estimated shares">

The formula averages over the missing indicators given the known ones, but the
SAGE estimator samples them from their marginal distribution in practice, so
with correlated indicators it computes the interventional version of this game.

Suppose that the linear model is the true conditional mean, the loss is squared
error and the indicators are jointly Gaussian, so that the conditional mean
given any subset is also linear. Then refitted explained variance, conditional
variance and conditional SAGE (with exact Gaussian conditional means) give the
same population shares once their normalisations match. Estimated global
shares can still differ because of training versus evaluation samples,
estimation error or misspecification. The diagram at the start of section 4
is close to these conditions: its indicators are lognormal and its P&L slightly
concave in volatility, so its three global games give the same split after
rounding, though not the same values for one indicator alone.

</Optional>

These shares measure predictive information, not realised P&L. A ranking is
read together with its game, its treatment of missing indicators and its
baseline.

### 4.3 Importance needs a comparison under the null

A model fitted to noise still returns shares, and one of them is the largest.
The top share therefore needs a comparison with the top share obtained when the
P&L is shuffled in blocks, which keeps its time dependence. That comparison says
whether any indicator predicts the outcome.

<Optional title="Fair competitors and other importance measures" subtitle="what the shuffle tests, simpler measures, knockoffs, null comparisons in interpretability">

Shuffling the whole response, one observation at a time or in blocks, does not
test whether one indicator adds information when others already predict the
outcome. If the finding is that one indicator has the largest share, a null
band computed for that indicator alone ignores that it was picked as the top;
the top share on shuffled responses accounts for that choice.

Simpler importance measures make different comparisons:

- a single-indicator fit can give high scores to several substitutes;
- dropping an indicator and refitting may barely hurt, because another replaces it;
- permuting a column without refitting can create combinations absent from the joint distribution.

These quantities need not add to a common total.

Shuffling one indicator creates an unfair competitor for it, because the shuffle
removes its correlations with the other indicators. Knockoffs
[@barberControllingFalseDiscovery2015] give each indicator a fair competitor. A
knockoff is an artificial variable with the same correlations with the other
indicators as the original, built without using the outcome. An indicator is
kept only if it clearly beats its knockoff. Under Barber and Candès's
assumptions, their Knockoff+ rule controls the false discovery rate: the
expected fraction of kept indicators whose true coefficient is zero. The method
selects variables and allocates no profit.

Their construction is fixed-X: a Gaussian linear model with a fixed design and,
in their basic construction, $n\geq 2p$ observations for $p$ indicators. @candesPanningGoldModelX2018 drop the linear
model: they assume that the joint distribution of the indicators is known, and
the link to the outcome can be nonlinear. Both versions assume independent
observations, so the guarantee does not cover serially dependent intraday
errors.

Attribution needs a null comparison even without variable selection.
@melouxDeadSalmonsAI2025 argue for explicit null comparisons in the
interpretability of neural networks; their own experiments are probing tasks,
not Shapley attribution.

</Optional>

### 5.1 A reference price for the ledger

**Question.** At what horizon does the price move after a fill stop reverting?
The fill records add up exactly, but their split into spread earned and
inventory P&L ([section 2.5](#25-spread-and-inventory-a-market-makers-pl))
depends on the reference price. Desks measure markouts at growing horizons: 1 s, 10 s, 1 min,
5 min and longer. The horizon at which the average markout stops reverting
sets the reference price that separates spread earned from inventory gains and
losses.

**First experiment.** We simulate a market maker whose fills move the price
permanently by a set amount, which is the information its counterparties
trade on, and transitorily by an amount that reverts. Because the permanent
impact is planted, we know what the market maker loses to it. We compute the
markouts at growing horizons, find the horizon at which they stop reverting,
and check that the price move up to that horizon recovers the planted impact.

**What would stop us.** The tolerance is fixed before the first run, for
example ±10% of the planted impact. We stop if the price move up to the
horizon at which markouts stop reverting misses the planted impact by more
than the tolerance. Spread and inventory P&L would then still add up without
telling the desk what it loses to better-informed counterparties.

<span id="ledger-data"></span>

**Data.** Each fill's time, side, price, size and status; own and market quotes;
inventory and hedges; fees and rebates.

<Optional title="The simulated model and the reference prices" subtitle="informed traders, permanent and transitory impact, what we report">

**Simulated model.** The simulation follows the classical models of trading
with informed counterparties, such as Kyle's
[@bouchaudTradesQuotesPrices2018, chap. 15] and Glosten and Milgrom's: a dealer
loses on its trades with informed traders, and each trade moves the price
permanently by the information it reveals.
Menkveld [@menkveldHighFrequencyTrading2013, pp. 21–23] splits midprice changes into a
permanent part, which reflects information, and a transitory part, which
reverts; he estimates this split with a state-space model, not per fill. The simulation plants both
parts, so the markout curve can be checked against a known permanent impact.

**Reference prices.** Menkveld's accounting and the decision-time paper
portfolio of @peroldImplementationShortfallPaper1988 use different references,
and neither says which one to use when quotes are revised continuously. We
reconcile the same fills against the midprice just before the fill, a
consolidated midprice and the midprices at each markout horizon, keeping total
wealth, hedge P&L, fees and rebates fixed. We report spread and inventory P&L
per fill and per unit traded, with their variation across repeated
simulations. A later midprice also contains unrelated moves; the planted
impact checks that the average markout curve separates the permanent part
from them.

</Optional>

### 5.2 Signals: how much each one earned

**Question.** When can a cheap method replace full reruns, and how much does
each quantity move by chance? A signal attribution serves three uses, each with
its own quantity. For a report whose shares add up, and a policy we can rerun,
we choose as target the Shapley shares over reruns of the policy on subsets of
signals [@shapleyValueNpersonGames1953]. Dropping a
signal needs the P&L lost when it alone is removed, net of costs and measured
on days not used to build the policy. Resizing a signal needs the value of a
small change in its weight.

**Target.** We compute it from every rerun on the simulator: Shapley shares
above a declared reference policy, removal losses and marginal values.

**First experiment.** We measure how far each cheap method is from each target,
across spread, number of signals, correlation and constraints. Separately, we
compute 90% error bars on one history by resampling blocks of days. We then
check that these bars contain the target's average over many simulated
histories in about 90% of histories. This tells us whether one history is
enough to measure a share's chance variation. We report the sampling error of coalition reruns
separately, against that history's exact shares.

**What would stop us.** As in 5.1, the tolerances are fixed in advance, one per
target; for example, they could be 5 percentage points for a Shapley share, 5%
of the total P&L for a removal loss, and the right sign and a relative error
below 20% for a marginal value. We stop if no cheap method meets them near the
desk's settings; reruns would then be unavoidable. We also stop if the error
bars contain the target in fewer than 90% of histories, beyond simulation
noise.

<span id="signal-data"></span>

**Data.** Decision-time signals, positions, costs, risk settings, initial
inventory and a policy we can rerun. The aggregate P&L alone cannot recover what
the policy would have done without a signal. A rerun keeps the recorded market
path: it assumes fills at observed prices, or a declared fill model for passive
orders, and no market impact of the desk's own trades
[@bouchaudTradesQuotesPrices2018, p. 402]. We state that assumption with
every share we report.

<Optional title="Reference policy, cheap methods, comparison, many signals and execution">

**Reference policy.** The empty coalition is the declared reference policy. It
may still hedge or unwind initial inventory. The shares allocate the increment
above it. Section 3 set the reference policy to no trading, so the empty
coalition was worth zero there. Another reference policy, such as trading on a
reference forecast, changes only the Shapley shares. The removal losses and
marginal values compare runs that both keep the other signals, so they do not
depend on the reference policy.

**Cheap methods.** Section 3 gives two: the stand-alone proxy, and the
optimiser's split, which needs no rerun but requires the optimiser's equations
[@durinSignalwisePerformanceAttribution2014]. In the synthetic study of section
3.5, the proxy moves away from the others as spread, the number of signals or a
positive correlation between them increases. That study used the optimiser's
split as its benchmark, because its pieces add up exactly for that optimiser;
here it is one of the methods compared with the target.

**Comparison.** We compare residuals, reconciliation and distances between
shares, and their sensitivity to correlation and to binding constraints. We
first reproduce the case where Shapley and Durin agree (fixed risk aversion,
no constraints, no spread), then interpret the differences.

**Many signals.** Coalition reruns must then be sampled, or grouped as in
section 3.4. A
regression estimator with an accuracy guarantee
[@muscoProvablyAccurateShapley2025] helps set the rerun budget. FastSHAP
[@jethaniFastSHAPRealTimeShapley2022] instead trains a model to predict the
shares directly.

**Signals in the execution schedule.** With a signal, the optimal execution
schedule splits into a liquidation part plus a signal part
[@lehalleIncorporatingSignalsOptimal2019, p. 9, Eq. 2.9]. Its impact cost,
quadratic in the schedule, then contains a cross term between the two parts
(our derivation). We propose to share that cross term between the two parts
with a stated coalition rule.

</Optional>

### 5.3 State indicators and opaque policies

- **State indicators.** Once the reference price is fixed, we relate spread and inventory P&L to state indicators such as the quoted spread, volatility and market order flow, with the Shapley games of section 4. These shares divide predictive information about each part, not realised P&L [@covertUnderstandingGlobalFeature2020], and must hold on later days.
- **Opaque policies.** Rule-based and learned policies have no optimality equations to use. We would train a surrogate model that predicts the policy's positions from its inputs, check that it reproduces the policy on held-out cases, and only then attribute through it. Removing a signal from the surrogate's inputs needs a removal operator, and each operator defines a different attribution (section 3.3). Even then, we would be assuming that the surrogate's attributions carry over to the policy.

<Optional title="What each extension needs" subtitle="data, and sources on opaque policies in practice">

**State indicators.** This needs the reference price and markout horizon of
5.1, and the indicators as known at each decision.

**Opaque policies.** Black-box trading in practice includes rule-based
automation; it need not mean a learned position-taking policy.
@gueantReinforcementLearningMethods2023 [p. 200] reviews reinforcement learning
for trading and finds that published applications so far only show
feasibility.
@kissellAlgorithmicTradingMethods2021 describes a network that gives an
optimiser a starting point; the optimiser still runs to find the solution.

</Optional>
